HS codes: the basics importers need

The Harmonized System (HS) is an internationally standardized numbering system, maintained by the World Customs Organization, that classifies traded goods so customs authorities worldwide can apply consistent duties and collect trade statistics. The first six digits of an HS code are shared globally; countries then add two to four more digits to create their own national tariff line, which is what actually determines the duty rate and any extra requirements at the border.

For an importer, getting the classification right matters for three reasons: it sets the duty rate you'll pay, it determines whether any additional permits or inspections apply, and a mismatch between your declared code and what customs believes is correct can trigger delays, reassessment, or penalties on future shipments too.

The typical HS code for bentonite cat litter

Bentonite is a naturally occurring clay mineral, and unrefined or minimally processed clays are classified under Chapter 25 of the Harmonized System, which covers "salt, sulphur, earths and stone, plaster, lime and cement." Within that chapter, heading 2508 covers "other clays," with bentonite typically falling under the 2508.10 subheading.

Some countries extend this into a more specific 8 or 10-digit national code for pet litter or animal bedding products, since customs authorities increasingly track pet care products separately from raw industrial clay. This is exactly why the code your freight forwarder used for a shipment last year isn't automatically the right one for a different destination country this year.

This section describes common classification practice, not a binding customs ruling. HS and national tariff codes are set and enforced by each country's own customs authority, and classification can depend on factors like packaging, declared end-use, and processing level. Always verify the applicable code with a licensed customs broker or your destination country's official tariff database before filing an import declaration.

What actually determines your import duty

Two shipments with the identical HS code can still attract different duty bills. The rate applied depends on several factors stacked together:

  • Destination country's tariff schedule. Each country sets its own duty rate per HS code, and these are revised periodically as trade policy changes.
  • Country of origin. Some countries apply different rates — sometimes zero — to goods originating from specific trading partners under a trade agreement, versus the standard "most favored nation" rate applied to everyone else.
  • Declared customs value. Duty is usually calculated as a percentage of the shipment's customs value, typically the CIF value (cost, insurance, and freight), so how the invoice is structured affects the final duty amount.
  • Additional levies. Beyond the base import duty, many countries apply VAT, GST, or other consumption taxes on top, calculated on the duty-inclusive value.

Trade agreements and preferential rates

If VetPet Care's country of manufacture (India) has a free trade agreement or preferential tariff arrangement with your destination country, your shipment may qualify for a reduced or zero duty rate instead of the standard rate. Qualifying usually requires meeting the agreement's "rules of origin" — proving the product was substantially made or processed in the originating country — and providing a valid certificate of origin issued by the appropriate authority.

Since these agreements and the products they cover change over time, it's worth checking with your customs broker or destination country's trade ministry whether a current agreement applies to your specific HS code before assuming the standard rate is your only option.

Documents customs will usually ask for

Beyond the commercial invoice, packing list, and bill of lading every shipment needs, customs authorities and import partners commonly request documentation specific to a mineral-based consumer product like cat litter:

  • Certificate of Analysis (COA). Confirms the product's physical and chemical specifications, useful for both customs classification and quality assurance on the receiving end.
  • Safety Data Sheet (SDS). Documents composition, handling, and any hazard classification, which some destination markets require for mineral products regardless of how low-risk the material actually is.
  • Certificate of origin. Required to claim any preferential duty rate under a trade agreement.
  • Fumigation or phytosanitary certificate. Sometimes requested for the wooden pallets or jute packaging used in the shipment, not the litter itself.

Why a customs broker still matters

None of the information above replaces a licensed customs broker in your destination country. Tariff schedules, trade agreements, and documentation requirements change, and a broker who works your specific port and product category daily will catch country-specific quirks — like a local tax that applies only to packaged pet products, or a labeling rule that has nothing to do with the HS code itself — long before they become a hold-up at the border.

The most efficient importers treat classification as a conversation between their supplier and their broker, not a one-time lookup. Frequently asked questions

What is the HS code for cat litter?
Bentonite cat litter is most commonly classified under Harmonized System heading 2508 (clays), typically at the 2508.10 subheading for bentonite, since it is an unrefined natural clay mineral. The exact national tariff code (the 8 to 10 digit extension beyond the 6-digit HS heading) varies by country, so buyers should confirm the precise code with their customs broker or national tariff schedule before shipping.
Is import duty on cat litter the same in every country?
No. Duty rates are set by each country's own tariff schedule and can differ significantly even for goods sharing the same HS heading, and they can also depend on the product's declared value, country of origin, and any trade agreement between the origin and destination countries.
Do I need a Certificate of Analysis for customs clearance?
Many customs authorities and import partners request a Certificate of Analysis and Safety Data Sheet to confirm the product's composition, especially for a mineral-based product like bentonite clay. Requirements vary by destination, so it's worth confirming with your customs broker whether these documents are mandatory or simply good practice for smoother clearance.
Can a trade agreement reduce the duty on cat litter imports?
Yes, in many cases. If the exporting and importing countries share a free trade agreement or preferential tariff arrangement, and the shipment meets the agreement's rules of origin, the applicable duty rate can be reduced or waived. A certificate of origin is usually required to claim this benefit.
